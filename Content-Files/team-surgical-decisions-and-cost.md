# Your Orders, Your Episode: How Surgical Decisions Drive TEAM Cost

Under TEAM, every Medicare claim for 30 days after your patient's discharge counts against a single target price. The anchor hospitalization is largely fixed once the patient is on the table. What happens after that — where they go, what equipment they receive, whether they come back — is determined almost entirely by decisions your team makes. Not case management. Not the hospitalist. Not administration. Your team.

This module maps five specific clinical decisions to their dollar consequences under TEAM. Each one is yours to make. Each one has a measurable cost range. Taken together, they represent the difference between an episode that performs and one that doesn't.

[PEARL] Surgeons control approximately 90% of the cost of care through clinical decision-making and orders, while receiving roughly 12% of the total episode payment. That leverage cuts both ways: it is the reason surgical leadership in TEAM programs matters, and the reason your documentation and discharge decisions have consequences that extend well beyond your fee.

## The Cost Context: What's at Stake by Procedure

Before the five decisions, a frame. Post-acute costs are not evenly distributed across TEAM procedures, which means the decisions below carry different weights depending on what you operate on.

| Procedure | Post-acute share of total episode | Readmission cost (avg) |
|-----------|----------------------------------|------------------------|
| SHFFT (hip fracture) | ~38–54% | ~$15,100 |
| LEJR (joint replacement) | ~21–27% | ~$15,100 |
| Major Bowel | ~21% | ~$15,100 |
| Spinal Fusion | ~18% | ~$15,100 |
| CABG | ~14% | ~$15,100 |

For SHFFT, more than half the episode cost happens after you close. For CABG, post-acute is a smaller share — but readmission is just as expensive and more frequent. The decisions below apply across all five procedures; the relative weight of each shifts by procedure type.

## Decision 1: Where Does This Patient Go After Discharge?

**The decision:** Home with home health, outpatient PT, SNF, or IRF.

**Why it's yours:** The discharge disposition order originates with the surgical or attending team. Case management executes it. The physician's clinical judgment — documented in the discharge summary — is the rationale that determines, defends, and directs where the patient goes.

**What it costs:**

| Discharge destination | Typical Medicare cost to episode |
|-----------------------|----------------------------------|
| Home with outpatient PT | ~$800–$2,000 |
| Home with home health | ~$2,000–$4,000 |
| Skilled Nursing Facility (14 days) | ~$7,000–$12,000 |
| Inpatient Rehabilitation Facility (10–14 days) | ~$18,000–$25,000 |

Every dollar in this table comes out of the same pool that determines whether your hospital earns a reconciliation payment or owes one. A patient discharged to home with home health costs the episode roughly $20,000 less than a patient admitted to IRF. That differential is not a billing curiosity — it is direct episode exposure.

Home health is the clear winner in bundled joint care. It costs less, patients tend to prefer it, and early-discharge-to-home plans cut inpatient days that drive up episode costs. For most elective joint replacement patients with adequate home support, home health with physical therapy is clinically equivalent to SNF at a fraction of the cost. Both BPCI program participants who achieved the most savings — reducing episode costs by 17–18% — did so almost entirely in the post-discharge phase, by decreasing discharges to institutional post-acute care and reducing SNF length of stay.

**What justifies SNF:** A patient who cannot safely manage at home — due to functional limitations, fall risk, inadequate home support, or wound care complexity — genuinely needs SNF-level care. Document that clinical rationale explicitly. "Patient unable to ambulate independently, lives alone without caregiver support, requires daily wound care — SNF placement medically necessary" is defensible and accurate. "SNF per patient preference" or "complex medical history" is not sufficient clinical justification under TEAM scrutiny.

**Patient rights and informed choice:** Patients have the right to choose their post-acute setting. What changes under TEAM is the obligation to present an informed choice, not a default. A patient told "you'll go to Sunridge Rehab" has not been given a choice. A patient told "most people in your situation go home with a physical therapist coming to the house — here is what that looks like, and here is what a rehab facility would look like" has been given one. Informed patients, given accurate information, choose home at higher rates than patients given no information.

[PHRASING]
❌ "Discharge to SNF — complex medical history, multiple comorbidities." — Comorbidities do not justify SNF. Functional limitations and care needs do.
✅ "Patient ambulating 75 feet with walker on POD2, tolerating PT twice daily, adequate home support confirmed. Discharge to home with home health PT ordered."

## Decision 2: Does This Patient Need IRF?

**The decision:** Whether to certify a patient for inpatient rehabilitation facility admission.

**Why it's yours:** Medicare requires physician certification that the patient requires intensive rehabilitation (≥3 hours of active therapy per day), continued medical supervision by a physician, and coordinated multidisciplinary care. In most hospitals, the attending surgeon or hospitalist signs this certification. That signature is the gating document for a $18,000–$25,000 Medicare claim.

**The clinical standard:** IRF is designed for patients recovering from stroke, traumatic brain injury, spinal cord injury, and severe burns — conditions requiring hospital-level medical management alongside intensive rehabilitation. For these patients, IRF is the right setting and the right cost. For patients who need rehabilitation but not daily physician supervision and not three hours of active therapy, SNF or home health is the appropriate — and far less expensive — setting.

The key distinction is not how sick the patient is. It is whether they need the specific intensity that defines an IRF admission. A patient with obesity, heart failure, and diabetes who had an uncomplicated total knee replacement does not automatically need IRF. Their comorbidities make them complex — but the question for IRF appropriateness is whether they require intensive rehab and daily physician supervision, not whether their problem list is long.

CJR experience showed this clearly: when hospitals had a financial incentive to examine their IRF referral patterns, many referrals that had been reflexive turned out to be SNF-appropriate or home-appropriate. The patients did not suffer. The episodes cost less.

**The financial magnitude at scale:** In a program doing 200 LEJR cases annually, if 20% of patients are going to IRF when home health is clinically appropriate, that is 40 episodes carrying a $15,000–$20,000 cost differential each — $600,000 to $800,000 in annual episode overspend attributable to a single discharge pattern.

[PHRASING]
❌ "Patient deconditioned and elderly, recommend IRF for intensive rehabilitation." — Deconditioning and age alone do not meet IRF criteria.
✅ "Patient requires intensive inpatient rehabilitation: unable to tolerate >1 hour of PT daily in current condition, requires daily physician management of [specific condition], coordinated multidisciplinary care medically necessary. IRF certified."

[DENIAL-RISK] IRF admissions are audited by Medicare. The three-part criteria — intensive therapy, physician supervision, coordinated care — must all be documented in the certification. An IRF admission that is reversed on audit recaptures the full claim value against the episode.

## Decision 3: What Does Your Discharge Summary Tell the SNF?

**The decision:** What you write — or don't write — in the discharge summary for patients going to SNF.

**Why it's yours:** The admitting SNF team reads your discharge summary. It is the primary clinical handoff. If it tells them when the patient should go home, they manage toward that target. If it doesn't, they default to caution and extended stays.

**How this controls SNF length of stay:** The most common cause of prolonged SNF stays is ambiguous or absent discharge criteria. SNF staff are cautious professionals managing liability. Without explicit milestones, "when can this patient go home?" has no answer, so they err toward longer stays. CJR was associated with a 0.76-day reduction in average SNF length of stay and nearly $500 less in SNF spending per episode — roughly correlated with hospitals that started writing better discharge criteria as part of bundle management.

A 14-day SNF stay for joint replacement costs Medicare $7,000–$12,000. An 18-day stay costs $3,000–$5,000 more. The difference is often not clinical complexity — it is whether anyone told the SNF what the endpoint looks like.

**What to write:** Functional discharge criteria should be specific, measurable, and tied to your clinical pathway. Not "when patient is ready" or "when patient meets SNF criteria for discharge." Those phrases mean nothing actionable to the SNF team.

[PHRASING]
❌ "Discharge to SNF for continued recovery and physical therapy. Follow up with surgeon in 2 weeks." — No functional milestones. SNF has no target.
✅ "Discharge to SNF for continued rehabilitation. Goals for transition to home: independent ambulation 100 feet with assistive device, ability to manage one flight of stairs, wound intact without signs of infection, adequate home support confirmed. Expected SNF duration 7–10 days pending PT progression."

**Beyond LOS — SNF selection matters too.** SNF quality varies significantly in readmission rates, discharge-to-home rates, and LOS by procedure type. A facility that performs well for orthopedic cases may perform poorly for CABG patients. Hospitals that analyzed their referral patterns and built preferred SNF networks — steering patients to higher-performing facilities based on outcomes data — reduced SNF LOS and readmission rates without restricting patient access. This is a case management and administration function, but surgeons who know which local SNFs have strong orthopedic or cardiac rehab programs make better referrals.

## Decision 4: What Happens in the First 10 Days After Discharge?

**The decision:** Whether there is a structured post-discharge contact protocol and early follow-up, and what the escalation pathway is when something goes wrong.

**Why it's yours:** The surgical team defines the post-discharge care structure. Whether there is a 48-hour nurse phone call, when the first office visit is, and what number the patient calls at 10pm with a wound question — these are clinical program decisions, not administrative ones. The surgical team sets the protocol; others execute it.

**The cost of the readmission you prevent:** The average readmission in a TEAM episode costs ~$15,100 and lands entirely against the episode target. It also triggers the quality adjustment — the Hybrid Hospital-Wide All-Cause Readmission Measure modifies reconciliation by up to ±10% across all episodes. One readmission is a cost event. A pattern of readmissions is a quality penalty on top of the cost.

Readmission causes differ by procedure, and the timing pattern matters for how you design surveillance:

**LEJR:** Surgical readmissions (wound infection, joint instability, DVT/PE) occur earlier — mean 18 days post-discharge. Medical readmissions (cardiac, urinary retention, ileus) occur later — mean 33 days. Early contact should screen for wound and surgical issues; later follow-up should screen for medical decompensation.

**SHFFT:** Medical readmission dominates — cardiac events, pneumonia, UTI, delirium. These patients need post-acute facilities capable of managing medical complexity, not just rehabilitation. The discharge summary should specify the patient's medical management needs alongside rehabilitation goals.

**CABG:** 30-day readmission rate is approximately 12.9%. Most common causes are infection and sepsis, cardiac arrhythmia, congestive heart failure, respiratory complications, and pleural effusion. Pleural effusion develops predictably after CABG — it is not a surprise complication, and a structured 7–10 day follow-up with imaging is the intervention that catches it before it becomes a readmission. Many CABG readmissions represent conditions that were developing at discharge and were not addressed before the patient left.

**Major Bowel:** Ileus, bowel obstruction, anastomotic complications, dehydration, and ostomy complications dominate. Ostomy patients have particularly high readmission rates from complications and inadequate self-care. A dedicated ostomy nurse follow-up call at 48–72 hours is among the highest-yield single interventions in this population.

**Spinal Fusion:** Wound complications, surgical site infection, hardware complications, and VTE. High VTE risk makes anticoagulation management and early mobility protocols relevant.

**The highest-yield post-discharge interventions:**

A nurse phone call at 48–72 hours is the single most consistently effective readmission prevention intervention across all surgical episode types. The call confirms wound care, medication adherence, and warning signs — and catches confusion about follow-up appointments before it becomes a no-show that delays recognition of a problem.

Early surgical follow-up at 7–10 days — not 2–3 weeks — is the clinical checkpoint that catches complications in the window when they are still manageable. Wound issues caught at day 7 are treated with antibiotics and wound care. The same issues caught at day 21 via readmission are treated with debridement and a $15,000 hospitalization.

A clear escalation pathway — a specific phone number, answered by a nurse who can triage — makes it easier for patients to call than to go to the ED. Most ED visits by surgical patients in the 30-day window become admissions. Most of those admissions were for problems that a triage nurse call could have managed.

[PEARL] The single most important post-discharge decision is structural: make it easier for your patient to call you than to call 911. A patient with a wound question at 9pm who has a direct nurse contact number will call. The same patient with no number will go to the ED. Most of those visits become the $15,100 admission that erases your episode margin.

## Decision 5: Should This Patient Have Surgery Now?

**The decision:** Whether to proceed with elective surgery or delay for optimization.

**Why it's yours:** No one else can make this call. Anesthesia can refuse, but they cannot reschedule. Case management can flag risk, but they cannot delay the case. The decision to operate, delay, and under what optimization conditions, belongs to the surgeon.

**Why it's the highest-leverage decision in the episode:** Every downstream cost — SNF, IRF, readmission, DME, post-discharge nursing — is downstream of a patient who was either well-prepared for surgery or wasn't. An A1c of 11 at the time of elective knee replacement predicts wound infection, slow healing, and extended recovery. Uncontrolled heart failure predicts prolonged hospital stay, SNF placement, and readmission. Active UTI before joint replacement predicts prosthetic joint infection — a catastrophic complication that costs tens of thousands of dollars and destroys the patient's outcome.

Under fee-for-service, there was no financial consequence at the episode level for proceeding with a poorly optimized patient. Under TEAM, the downstream costs of that decision accumulate in a single episode account. The incentive to optimize is now financial as well as clinical.

**Optimization thresholds worth standardizing for elective procedures:**

| Variable | Threshold to consider delay |
|----------|-----------------------------|
| HbA1c | >8.0 (consider; >9.0 strong reason to delay) |
| BMI | >40 — assess functional mobility and home support |
| Active infection | Any active site infection — delay until resolved |
| Decompensated heart failure | Delay until euvolemic and stable |
| Uncontrolled hypertension | >180/110 persistent — optimize before proceeding |
| Nutritional status | Albumin <3.0 or severe malnutrition — nutrition consult, consider delay |
| Social support | No caregiver at home for high-complexity patients — confirm support plan before scheduling |

[NOTE] Optimization thresholds should be established as a program-level policy, not applied inconsistently by individual surgeon preference. A shared protocol gives the surgical team clinical cover to delay cases, gives patients clear expectations, and produces outcomes data that demonstrates the program's commitment to appropriate patient selection.

**The TEAM financial logic:** A patient with an A1c of 11 who develops a wound infection and readmits generates approximately $15,100 in readmission costs plus extended home health and additional DME. A one-to-two month delay for glycemic optimization costs the patient nothing clinical and costs the episode nothing. The math strongly favors the delay.

[PHRASING]
❌ "Patient eager to proceed, will optimize postoperatively." — Postoperative optimization does not reduce perioperative and episode risk.
✅ "Elective LEJR deferred pending glycemic optimization. A1c currently 10.2. Target A1c <8.0 before proceeding. Patient referred to PCP for diabetes management. Reschedule in 6–8 weeks pending lab results."

## A Note on DME Orders

DME is not a major cost driver relative to the decisions above, but it deserves mention because it is entirely order-driven. Under TEAM, all Part B DME claims in the 30-day episode window count against the target.

Walker, raised toilet seat, shower chair, and cane are appropriate and expected. The discipline is on the edges: wound VAC duration should be actively managed and discontinued when healing is confirmed; CPM devices for TKA have limited evidence for routine use and should be ordered when specifically indicated; TENS and nerve stimulator units should be documented for medical necessity if ordered. A wound VAC running 45 days beyond clinical need is not a large cost item individually — but at program volume, reflexive and indefinite DME orders are a consistent minor drag that clinical discipline can eliminate.

## Quick Reference: Five Decisions and Their Episode Cost

| Decision | Low-cost outcome | High-cost outcome | Cost differential |
|----------|-----------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Discharge destination | Home + home health (~$2,000–$4,000) | IRF (~$18,000–$25,000) | Up to ~$22,000 per episode |
| IRF certification | Not certified (appropriate) | Certified when SNF/home sufficient | $15,000–$20,000 per episode |
| Discharge summary quality | Specific milestones → 7–10 day SNF stay | Vague → 14–21 day SNF stay | $3,000–$8,000 per episode |
| Post-discharge follow-up | 48-hr call + 7-day visit → prevented readmission | No contact → avoidable readmission | ~$15,100 per readmission |
| Pre-op optimization | Optimized patient, clean episode | Unoptimized → wound infection, readmission | $15,000–$30,000+ per complication |

**Readmission causes by procedure — what to screen for:**

| Procedure | Early window (days 1–21) | Late window (days 22–30) |
|-----------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| LEJR | Wound infection, DVT/PE, joint complication | Medical decompensation, cardiac, urinary |
| SHFFT | Delirium, fall, medical complication | Pneumonia, UTI, cardiac |
| CABG | Arrhythmia, pleural effusion, wound infection | CHF, respiratory, sepsis |
| Major Bowel | Ileus, leak, wound, ostomy complication | Dehydration, obstruction, sepsis |
| Spinal Fusion | Wound, hardware, neurological | VTE, infection |

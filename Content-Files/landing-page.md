## Hero

# Your documentation is worth more than you think.

Your hospital now receives a single bundled payment for each episode of care, and Medicare calculates this payment based on the severity of illness documented for your patients. The only way insurance companies can assess how sick the patients are is through the documentation you provide. This playbook outlines what this means in practice and offers guidance on what to write.

### I'm a Surgeon

Your orders and your discharge summary are the two highest-leverage documents in the episode. Where you send a patient after surgery — home, SNF, or rehab — determines a large share of episode cost. What you write in the discharge summary determines whether your patient's clinical complexity is captured or invisible to the payment system.

This playbook tells you which diagnoses to name, how to phrase them, and what a compliant discharge summary looks like for each of the five procedures.

<!-- RoleSelectorCard: Medicine Physician / Hospitalist -->
### I'm a Medicine Physician or Hospitalist

You manage the comorbidities. That makes you the most important documentation author in the building for TEAM purposes. Sepsis, AKI, encephalopathy, malnutrition, heart failure — the diagnoses that move DRG tiers are almost always documented (or missed) by medicine, not surgery.

This playbook tells you exactly which conditions to name, how specifically to name them, and why "CHF" and "confusion" are not diagnoses for coding purposes.

**Start here →** CDI Fundamentals, then the CC/MCC Reference. The phrasing examples are written for your notes, not surgical notes.

<!-- RoleSelectorCard: APP -->
### I'm an APP

You write progress notes and often the discharge summary. The discharge summary is the final opportunity to capture every diagnosis that was managed during the stay — and the one document coders rely on most. An incomplete or vague discharge summary leaves complexity uncaptured regardless of what appeared in earlier notes.

This playbook gives you the phrasing, the checklists, and the before/after examples you need to write a discharge summary that accurately reflects what happened.

## What This Is About — In 60 Seconds

Medicare now pays for joint replacements, hip fracture surgeries, bypass operations, spinal fusions, and bowel resections as bundled episodes. One payment covers the surgery, the hospital stay, and 30 days of care after discharge — including any readmissions, SNF stays, rehab, and home health.

That payment is set in advance based on a target price. Your hospital keeps the difference if total costs come in under target. Your hospital owes money if costs exceed target.

Two things directly affect that target price:

**1. DRG tier.** The severity tier of the anchor hospitalization is determined by secondary diagnoses — CCs and MCCs. "CHF" is not an MCC. "Acute systolic heart failure" is. Same clinical situation, different DRG, different payment, different episode performance.

**2. HCC risk adjustment.** Chronic conditions documented in the six months before surgery adjust the target price upward for complex patients. A patient with heart failure, COPD, and diabetes with neuropathy generates a higher target price than the same patient with no documented comorbidities — as long as those conditions are documented.

Neither of these requires documenting anything that isn't clinically present. Both require documenting what is present with enough precision for the coding system to represent it accurately.

That is the whole thing. That is what this playbook is for.

## One More Thing

This playbook is not about upcoding. It is not about adding diagnoses that are not clinically supported. Every phrasing example, every checklist item, every "write this instead of that" assumes the condition is present and being treated.

The problem CDI programs exist to solve is not fraud — it is the gap between what clinicians know and what the medical record says. A patient with AKI who is getting IV fluids and nephrology consults has AKI. A discharge summary that says "creatinine elevated, monitoring" does not say AKI. The condition is real. The documentation does not reflect it. That is the gap.

Closing that gap is accurate medicine and accurate documentation. It is also what makes the payment system work the way it was designed to.

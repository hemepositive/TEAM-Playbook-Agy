# What Is TEAM?

Starting January 1, 2026, CMS changed how Medicare pays for five common surgical procedures at hundreds of hospitals across the country. If your hospital is in a selected region, you are participating whether you opted in or not. This module explains what changed, what it means for your patients, and why your documentation is one of the most direct levers you have.

[PEARL] TEAM is mandatory. It is not a voluntary pilot. There is no opt-out for hospitals in selected geographic areas. Every Medicare fee-for-service patient you operate on in one of the five covered procedures is part of a TEAM episode — automatically, starting with your first case in 2026.

---

<!-- Section: WhatIsTeam -->
## The Core Idea in Plain Language

Before TEAM, Medicare paid for each service separately: the surgery, the hospitalization, the SNF stay, the home health visits, the readmission — each as its own transaction. Hospitals and physicians were paid regardless of how efficiently or effectively the episode unfolded overall.

TEAM bundles all of that into a single episode. Each episode includes the anchor hospitalization and a 30-day post-acute period, starting on the first day of discharge for inpatient procedures or the day of the procedure for outpatient cases. Medicare adds up every non-excluded claim during that window — inpatient, outpatient, SNF, home health, rehab, readmissions, physician fees, labs, DME — and compares the total to a pre-set target price.

If actual costs are below the target and quality standards are met, the hospital may receive a reconciliation payment. If costs exceed the target, the hospital may owe CMS money, unless they are in a non-risk or limited-risk track.

That is the model. Spend less than the target, with good quality: you benefit. Spend more: you owe. Your documentation affects both sides of that equation.

<!-- Section: WhichHospitals -->
## Which Hospitals Are Affected

Acute care hospitals paid under IPPS and OPPS located in selected Core-Based Statistical Areas (CBSAs) across the U.S. are required to participate. Selected CBSAs were published in the FY 2025 IPPS final rule and capture a mix of hospitals with different value-based care experiences.

Hospitals that participated through the end of BPCI Advanced or CJR were eligible to voluntarily opt in; everyone else in a selected CBSA is mandatory. If you are unsure whether your hospital is in a selected region, your hospital administration will know — this is not something a physician needs to determine independently.

[NOTE] TEAM covers only Original Medicare (Part A and B) beneficiaries. Patients with Medicare Advantage, managed Medicare, or Medicare eligibility due to end-stage renal disease are excluded from TEAM episodes. Not every patient you operate on is in a TEAM episode — but for those who are, you will not know at the time of surgery.

<!-- Section: FiveProcedures -->
## The Five Procedures

Episodes of focus are Lower Extremity Joint Replacement, Surgical Hip Femur Fracture Treatment, Spinal Fusion, Coronary Artery Bypass Graft, and Major Bowel Procedure.

In brief:

**Lower Extremity Joint Replacement (LEJR)** — total hip, total knee, and total ankle arthroplasty. Both elective cases and urgent fracture cases are included. Triggered by MS-DRG 469, 470, 521, 522, or outpatient CPT codes 27130, 27447, 27702.

**Surgical Hip/Femur Fracture Treatment (SHFFT)** — operative treatment of hip and femur fractures in Medicare patients. Often a higher-risk, less elective population than LEJR.

**Spinal Fusion** — a wide family of procedures across cervical, thoracic, and lumbar regions, both anterior and posterior approaches. DRG family varies by level and complexity.

**Coronary Artery Bypass Graft (CABG)** — on-pump and off-pump CABG, including cases with concomitant valve or other cardiac procedures. DRG family determined by concomitant procedure.

**Major Bowel Procedure** — resections, ostomies, and related operations across colorectal, small bowel, and related structures. Includes both elective and emergency presentations.

## The Target Price: What It Is and How It's Set

The target price is the number your hospital's episode spending will be measured against. Understanding how it is built clarifies why documentation matters.

TEAM target prices are based on Medicare FFS episode spending across all hospitals in the hospital's geographic region during the baseline period, not the individual hospital's own spending history. Every participating hospital in a region starts from the same benchmark, regardless of its historical spending levels.

For each performance year, CMS establishes a three-year baseline period of historical clinical episodes. The baseline period rolls forward each year so that the most recent data is incorporated. For Performance Year 1 (calendar year 2026), the baseline is 2022–2024.

The formula for the hospital-specific target price adds several adjustments on top of that regional benchmark:

**Prospective trend factor** — adjusts for expected changes in spending between the baseline period and the performance year.

**Risk adjustment multiplier** — the critical one for documentation purposes. Risk adjustment at the beneficiary level includes age (four brackets), beneficiary economic risk using the Community Deprivation Index, and HCC count risk adjustment factor — for beneficiaries with 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4+ HCCs — found on a 180-day lookback ending one day prior to episode initiation.

**Discount factor** — represents Medicare's portion of savings: 1.5% for CABG and major bowel procedures, and 2% for LEJR, SHFFT, and spinal fusion. This is CMS's built-in margin — the target price is already set slightly below the expected spend to generate savings for the program.

**The documentation implication:** HCCs documented in the 180 days before surgery directly raise your patient's risk adjustment multiplier, which raises your target price, which gives you more room to operate within the bundle. A patient with four documented HCCs generates a higher target price than the same patient with no documented HCCs. If that patient's comorbidities are real but undocumented, the hospital absorbs the difference.

[NOTE] The risk adjustment lookback is 180 days prior to episode initiation. HCC documentation from ambulatory visits, pre-admission testing, and any inpatient encounter in the six months before surgery all count — not just the operative hospitalization itself.

## Quality Measures and Reconciliation

Hitting the target price is not enough on its own. Performance on quality measures can modify reconciliation payments by up to ±10%, making quality performance essential.

Three quality measures influence the final reconciliation amount: the Hybrid Hospital-Wide All-Cause Readmission Measure with Claims and EHR Data, the CMS Patient Safety and Adverse Events Composite, and — for LEJR — the Hospital-Level Total Hip and/or Total Knee Arthroplasty Patient Reported Outcome-Based Performance Measure (PRO-PM).

These measures are risk-adjusted using the same coded diagnosis data that drives DRG assignment. A hospital with a poorly documented patient population will appear to have worse risk-adjusted readmission and complication rates than their clinical performance actually warrants — because the benchmark for "expected" outcomes was built on an underdocumented comorbidity profile.

This is the second place documentation affects TEAM performance: not just the target price, but the quality benchmark against which outcomes are measured.

<!-- Section: ParticipationTracks -->
## Participation Tracks

TEAM has graduated risk through different participation tracks to accommodate different levels of risk and reward and allow participants to ease into full-risk participation.

| Track | Who | Financial Risk |
|-------|-----|---------------|
| Track 1 | All hospitals, PY1; safety-net hospitals, PY1–3 | Upside only — up to 10% bonus, no penalty |
| Track 2 | Safety-net, rural, Medicare-dependent, sole community, PY2–5 | ±5% cap |
| Track 3 | All hospitals, all years | ±20% cap |

In practical terms: in 2026 (Performance Year 1), no hospital owes CMS money. Every participating hospital is in Track 1 with upside-only risk. Starting in Performance Year 2, most hospitals move to Track 3 unless they qualify for Track 2 protection. Track 3's 20% stop-loss represents meaningful financial exposure at volume.

[NOTE] Reconciliation occurs six months after each performance year ends, to allow for late claims. The financial consequence of a full year's episodes lands as a single payment or repayment roughly 18 months after the first case. This lag means the data and documentation decisions made today won't show up as a check or a bill until well into the following year.

## How TEAM Differs From BPCI and CJR

Many surgeons — particularly in orthopedics and cardiac surgery — have prior experience with bundled payment models. TEAM shares their structure but differs in important ways.

**BPCI (Bundled Payments for Care Improvement, 2013–2018) and BPCI Advanced (2018–2025)** were voluntary. Hospitals and physician groups opted in, selected which episode types to include, and could exit the program. BPCI had limited enforcement of care coordination standards. Participants with efficient practice patterns selected themselves in; others stayed out.

**CJR (Comprehensive Care for Joint Replacement, 2016–2025)** was mandatory for hip and knee replacement in selected MSAs and demonstrated that bundled payments could reduce costs without compromising outcomes in high-volume orthopedic procedures. CJR included post-acute care services for 90 days after discharge — a longer window than TEAM's 30 days.

**TEAM** is mandatory, covers five procedure families instead of one, uses a 30-day post-acute window, and incorporates HCC-based risk adjustment that directly links pre-episode documentation to the financial benchmark. The mandatory, multi-specialty scope means the program captures hospitals and surgeons who have never participated in value-based care before.

[NOTE] While orthopaedic surgeons' Part B payments represent only 12% of total LEJR reimbursement, surgeons control approximately 90% of the cost of care through clinical decision-making and orders. The same principle applies across the other four procedure families. The physician is the clinical decision-maker; the hospital carries the financial accountability. That relationship requires active collaboration — not passive coexistence.

## What This Means for Your Practice

TEAM does not change how you are individually paid. Your Part B fee-for-service payments continue as before. The model creates financial accountability at the hospital level, not the individual physician level.

What it changes is the context around your practice:

**Your documentation affects the target price.** HCCs documented in the 180 days before surgery raise the risk-adjusted benchmark. Underdocumented comorbidities lower it, making the episode look more expensive than it should. This is not a billing issue — it is a data accuracy issue that directly affects whether your hospital's episode performance looks good or bad.

**Your discharge documentation affects quality metrics.** Risk-adjusted readmission and complication rates depend on coded comorbidity data. If a patient readmits with a complication that was foreseeable given their clinical complexity, that readmission looks worse when the complexity wasn't documented.

**Your post-discharge decisions drive episode cost.** Discharge disposition — home, home with PT, SNF, inpatient rehab — is the largest variable cost in most episodes after the anchor hospitalization. Reducing unnecessary SNF placement, shortening SNF stays when appropriate, and ensuring patients transition to home with appropriate support are the primary levers most institutions pull.

**Your participation in care coordination matters.** Surgeon-led, multidisciplinary care has demonstrated the greatest level of improvement in overall patient outcomes, including decreases in length of stay, increased proportion of discharge to home, reduction in 90-day readmission rates and reduction in complication rates. Clinical leadership — not just clinical execution — is what moves the needle.

[PEARL] TEAM does not ask you to do less. It asks you to document what you already know, coordinate care you already believe in, and make discharge decisions you would make anyway for a well-informed patient. The model creates financial consequences for systemic failures that were previously invisible. Your documentation is how the system knows how sick your patients actually were.

## The Documentation Connection

The rest of this playbook goes deep on documentation mechanics. Here is the summary at the TEAM level:

**Before surgery (180-day HCC window):** Chronic conditions documented at any outpatient visit or pre-admission encounter contribute to the risk adjustment multiplier. Diabetes with complications, heart failure, COPD, CKD, dementia, morbid obesity, and many others raise the target price when accurately documented. The same conditions unrecorded lower it.

**During the hospitalization (DRG tier):** Secondary diagnoses — MCCs and CCs — determine the MS-DRG tier, which drives the hospital's per-episode payment for the anchor stay. The DRG itself is part of the episode cost calculation. A higher-tier DRG reflects a more complex patient and is the accurate code for that patient.

**At discharge (quality benchmarking):** The discharge summary is the last opportunity to ensure the full clinical picture is coded. Risk-adjusted quality measures use that coded data to set expected outcomes. An incomplete discharge summary produces a distorted quality benchmark.

None of this requires documenting things that aren't present. It requires documenting what is present with enough specificity that the coding system can represent it accurately. That is the goal of everything that follows in this playbook.
